---
title: Patient Autonomy
order: 4
description: Patients' right to make informed decisions about their own care — including the right to refuse treatment, understand alternatives, and participate meaningfully in clinical decisions.
---

Informed consent is a legal requirement and an ethical foundation. In practice, it often falls short: consent forms written in language that patients can't parse, decisions made in conditions of stress and time pressure, and clinical recommendations that reflect provider preferences as much as patient values. Genuine shared decision-making requires time, communication skill, translated materials, and a willingness to follow the patient's lead even when their choice differs from what the clinician would recommend.

Automation and clinical algorithms complicate autonomy in new ways. When a risk score shapes what options a clinician presents, or a protocol routes patients into care pathways without individual review, the patient's ability to make an informed choice is affected by decisions they're never aware of. Healthcare organizations that value patient autonomy need to think about where in the clinical workflow autonomy is actually exercised — and where it's quietly foreclosed before the patient enters the room.
